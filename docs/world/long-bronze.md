# The Long Bronze

**The one thing to know:** the Bronze Age Collapse never happened. Everything in this document is downstream of that.

**Dating:** Years After Kadesh (AK). 0 AK is 1259 BCE, the year Egypt and Hatti signed. Present day for the project is roughly 750 AK, which is about 510 BCE on our calendar.

**Notation:** `[real]` marks something that happened in our history or is attested in the archaeological record. Everything unmarked is invented. Keep this convention when you extend the setting — knowing which load-bearing walls are real is what stops the whole thing drifting into generic fantasy.

---

## 1. The divergence

`[real]` In 1259 BCE, Ramesses II of Egypt and Hattusili III of Hatti ended decades of war with a treaty of mutual defence, non-aggression, and extradition. Copies survive in Egyptian hieroglyphic and Akkadian cuneiform. It is the oldest peace treaty whose text we can still read from both sides.

`[real]` Between roughly 1200 and 1150 BCE, that world ended. Hattusa burned. Ugarit was destroyed and never reoccupied. The Mycenaean palaces burned. Egypt survived, diminished, and never again ran the Mediterranean.

**The change:** at the height of the crisis, the Kadesh defence clause is invoked rather than ignored. Egypt, Hatti, Ugarit, and Alashiya respond as one bloc instead of falling one at a time.

## 2. Why it held

Three mechanisms, none of them miraculous. If you need to invent more history later, build it on these.

**The clause already existed.** Nobody had to negotiate an alliance while cities were burning. The instrument was written, sworn, and decades old. Invoking it was a legal act, not a diplomatic achievement.

**Ugarit's harbour survived the first wave.** Copper from Alashiya and tin from the east kept moving through the worst decade. Everyone who stayed in the Concord kept eating and kept getting bronze. Everyone who didn't, didn't.

`[real]` **The raiders were absorbed, not defeated.** Egypt genuinely did settle some of the groups it called Sea Peoples as garrison troops and vassals. The Concord did the same thing deliberately, early, and at far larger scale. This is the single most consequential decision in the setting's history and it created a population that is still politically unresolved seven centuries later. See `factions.md`.

## 3. The oath problem

`[real]` The Treaty of Kadesh was sworn before a thousand gods of Hatti and a thousand gods of Egypt, both pantheons named as witnesses.

This makes the Concord a religious instrument rather than merely a political one. Breaking it is blasphemy in two theologies simultaneously, and by 300 AK in six. That is the load-bearing beam under 750 years of improbable stability.

It is also the setting's fault line. An oath that binds because it is sacred depends on most people never reading it closely. The treaty is short, specific, and concerned mostly with extradition and royal succession. It does not say most of what the Concord now claims it says.

**When the press arrives, everyone can read the treaty. That is the story.**

## 4. Tin, and why the map looks like that

`[real]` Bronze is roughly nine parts copper to one part tin. The Uluburun shipwreck, c. 1300 BCE, was carrying ingots in about that ratio along with goods from at least seven cultures.

`[real]` Copper is abundant and close. Cyprus alone can supply the eastern Mediterranean. Tin is rare and comes from the ends of the earth: Badakhshan in Afghanistan, Cornwall in Britain, and some poorer Anatolian deposits.

So a civilisation running on bronze needs a four-thousand-kilometre supply line for one of its two ingredients, and a short one for the other. That asymmetry is not a game designer's invention. It is the actual Bronze Age, and it is the best thing this setting has.

| Route | Source | Runs through | Controlled by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eastern Tin Road | Badakhshan | Elam, Mesopotamia, the Syrian desert | Assur, contested |
| Western Tin Road | Cornwall | Atlantic coast, Iberia, Tyrrhenian Sea | Intermediaries who lie about the source |
| Anatolian workings | Kestel and lesser sites | Overland to the plateau | Hatti, directly |
| Copper | Alashiya (Cyprus) | Short sea crossings | Alashiya, jealously |

Whoever holds tin holds everything. Whoever holds copper holds a lot and knows it.

## 5. Two scripts, two constituencies

This is the mechanism that makes early printing plausible, and it is the part most worth getting right.

`[real]` Ugarit used an alphabetic cuneiform of about thirty signs, in use by the thirteenth century BCE. It died when Ugarit was destroyed and was not rediscovered until 1929.

Here it does not die. Consequences compound for six hundred years.

**Temple script** — Akkadian cuneiform and Egyptian hieroglyphic. Hundreds of signs, years of training, a professional caste. Carries liturgy, treaty text, land title, royal annals. Slow, expensive, and controlled by people who like it that way.

**Road script** — the Ugaritic alphabet, expanded and standardised by merchants who needed it to work in six languages. Thirty-odd signs. Teachable to a merchant's son in a single season. Carries manifests, contracts, private letters, debt records, and gossip.

By 750 AK, literacy in temple script sits at maybe two percent. Literacy in road script in the port cities runs closer to a quarter of the free population.

**The press only works on road script.** You cannot economically cut blocks for six hundred signs. The technology has a side, and the side it takes is the merchants'. Every temple in the Concord understood this within a decade of the first press and none of them could do anything about it.

## 6. The press

`[real]` Cylinder seals were rolled across wet clay to authenticate documents and containers. Seal-carving was a mature, high-skill craft with centuries of accumulated technique.

Around 710 AK, block-cutters in the Ugarit merchant guilds work out how to carve flat relief blocks that reproduce a full standard contract form rather than a mark of ownership. The immediate application is boring and enormously profitable: bills of lading, credit notes, and template contracts, produced at speed and in identical copies.

Within a generation the technique reaches literary and religious text. Within two, there is a live and unresolved question about which copy of anything is authoritative, and the answer is no longer automatically the one in the temple.

## 7. Why iron stayed marginal

`[real]` Iron spread in our history partly because the collapse severed the tin routes. Iron ore is common; tin is not. Scarcity drove substitution.

Here the tin kept flowing, so iron never got its crisis. `[real]` Hatti was working iron by the thirteenth century BCE, and that continues, but as a frontier and agricultural metal: ploughshares, nails, cheap tools, the equipment of people who cannot afford bronze.

Bronze stays prestigious, and by 750 AK bronze metallurgy has several centuries of refinement that our timeline never got. Expect superb alloys, sophisticated casting, and a genuine technical conservatism among smiths who are very good at something the future does not want.

**Design note:** "advanced" should look strange here. Excellent bronze, indifferent iron, elaborate bureaucracy, and no real coinage until very late.

## 8. Society, briefly

`[real]` Value is reckoned in weighed silver and in grain, not coin. Coinage is a late and contested arrival.

`[real]` Credit, interest, and debt instruments are ancient and sophisticated. The Mesopotamian world had a functioning commercial law long before this period.

`[real]` The great merchant houses descend from the karum system, the Assyrian trading colonies at Kanesh, which ran on family firms, written correspondence, and extended credit.

`[real]` Ugarit's excavated merchant archives belonged to named individuals: Urtenu, Rapanu, Yabninu. In this timeline those houses were never buried, and their descendants still hold the ledgers. See `factions.md`.

## 9. Religion

Syncretic and layered. `[real]` Storm gods across the region were identified with one another early and easily — Baal, Teshub, Set, Adad are the same weather in different mouths.

Every Concord Assembly opens by reciting the Thousand and the Thousand, the divine witnesses to Kadesh. It takes most of a day and nobody has ever successfully proposed shortening it.

The temples' real power was never belief. It is that they hold the authoritative copies. A land dispute, a succession, a treaty obligation — all of it is settled by producing the correct tablet, and the correct tablet lives in a temple. This is a power that a press can dissolve in about forty years, and the temples know it.

## 10. What is different from our world, at a glance

| | Our timeline, c. 510 BCE | The Long Bronze, 750 AK |
|---|---|---|
| Dominant metal | Iron, widely | Bronze, still, and better than ours ever got |
| Writing | Alphabets spreading, Greek literacy rising | Two-tier: temple script and road script |
| Reproduction of text | Hand copying only | Block presses, forty years old, spreading fast |
| Political order | Persian empire, Greek poleis | The Concord: six seats, one 750-year treaty |
| Bronze Age states | Gone for six centuries | Continuous, unbroken, ossifying |
| Coinage | Establishing itself | Marginal and distrusted |
| The Sea Peoples | A destructive episode | A settled population without representation |

## 11. Deliberate gaps

Left open on purpose. Fill them when the project needs them and not before.

- What is happening east of Elam. Contact with the Indus and beyond is assumed but undocumented.
- The far west past Iberia. The Western Tin Road's actual origin is a commercial secret in-fiction and an unwritten one out of it.
- Whether the Concord ever tried to expand past the Aegean, and what it cost.
- The Nubian and Saharan south, which Egypt certainly deals with and this document ignores.
