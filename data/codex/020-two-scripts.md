Two ways of writing divide the world more surely than any border.

Temple script — the old cuneiform, the hieroglyphs — runs to hundreds of signs and years of training. It carries liturgy, treaty text, land title, and the annals of kings, and it is controlled by a professional caste who like it that way. Perhaps two people in a hundred can read it.

Road script is the Ugaritic alphabet: thirty-odd signs, standardised by merchants who needed it to work across six languages, teachable to a merchant's child in a single season. It carries manifests, contracts, debts, letters, and gossip. In the port cities, a quarter of the free population reads it.

The block press, when it came, could only economically speak road script — no one can cut blocks for six hundred signs. The technology chose a side the day it was invented. Every temple in the Concord understood this within a decade, and none of them could do anything about it.
