In the year the Concord counts as zero, Ramesses of Egypt and Hattusili of Hatti ended decades of war with a written instrument: mutual defence, non-aggression, extradition, sworn before a thousand gods of each side. Silver tablets were exchanged. It is the oldest peace treaty whose text can still be read from both sides, and in another history — the reader's history — a copy hangs in a glass case in a building full of diplomats who have mostly stopped reading it.

The treaty is short. It is specific. It concerns itself chiefly with extradition and royal succession, and it says almost nothing of what the Concord now claims it says. For seven centuries this did not matter, because the treaty was holy, and holy things are recited, not read.

Then the press arrived, and reading became cheap.
